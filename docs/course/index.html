01

Week 1 of 10

Sentence Control

Length, diction, and the two great sentence shapes: cumulative and periodic.

  1. Read Cormac McCarthy — "They rode on." (Blood Meridian)

    The cumulative sentence at full gallop — clause added to clause without mercy.

  2. Read Thomas Jefferson (Committee of Five) — Declaration of Independence - Self-Evident Truths

    The periodic sentence holds everything suspended until the main clause lands.

  3. Quiz Syntax & Word Order — 10 questions

    Aim for 7 of 10 before moving on; re-run it if the family fights back.

  4. Drill 1.1 The Length Dial

    Controlling sentence length at will.

  5. Drill 1.2 Saxon and Latin

    Toggling between Anglo-Saxon and Latinate diction.

  6. Drill 1.3 The Cumulative Builder

    Constructing right-branching (cumulative/loose) sentences.

02

Week 2 of 10

Repetition Structures

Anaphora, epistrophe, and their cousins: repetition that builds pressure instead of padding.

  1. Read Martin Luther King Jr. — "I Have a Dream"

    The most famous anaphora in American speech, building until the refrain feels inevitable.

  2. Read William Shakespeare — Macbeth "Tomorrow" Soliloquy

    Repetition drained of hope: the same word returning to say nothing changes.

  3. Quiz Repetition & Refrain — 10 questions

    Aim for 7 of 10 before moving on; re-run it if the family fights back.

  4. Drill 2.1 Anaphora Surge

    Beginning successive clauses with the same word or phrase.

  5. Drill 2.2 Epistrophe Lock

    Ending successive clauses with the same word or phrase.

  6. Drill 2.3 The Chain (Anadiplosis)

    Ending one clause with a word that begins the next.

  1. Read Charles Dickens — A Tale of Two Cities Opening

  2. Read Abraham Lincoln — Lincoln's Second Inaugural

    Balance used for mercy — matched clauses refusing vengeance.

  3. Quiz Balance & Opposition — 10 questions

    Aim for 7 of 10 before moving on; re-run it if the family fights back.

  5. Drill 3.2 Chiasmus Forge

  6. Drill 3.3 The Concession Pivot

    Genuinely conceding an opponent's strongest point, then turning it to your advantage.

    A dock light made to carry a whole national longing.

  2. Read William Butler Yeats — "The Second Coming"

Week 5 of 10

Rhythm and Conjunction

Asyndeton, polysyndeton, and the pulse of prose — what connectives do to time.

  1. Read Herman Melville — Moby-Dick Opening

    Clipped command melting into meandering ands — both connective strategies in three lines.

  2. Read Gwendolyn Brooks — "We Real Cool"

    The rhythm carries the entire argument — enjambment and omission at maximum compression.

  3. Quiz Omission & Flow — 10 questions

    Aim for 7 of 10 before moving on; re-run it if the family fights back.

  4. Drill 5.1 Asyndeton Rush

    Removing conjunctions to create speed, urgency, and force.

  5. Drill 5.2 Polysyndeton Weight

    Multiplying conjunctions to create weight, accumulation, and emotional density.

  6. Drill 5.3 The Conjunction Toggle

    Switching between asyndeton and polysyndeton to control pacing.

Week 6 of 10

  1. Read Martin Luther King Jr. — Letter from Birmingham Jail

    Argument under pressure — concession, address, and the periodic case for urgency.

  2. Read William Shakespeare — The Merchant of Venice - "Hath not a Jew eyes?"

    Rhetorical questions in parallel: identical structures asserting identical humanity.

  3. Quiz Address & Argument — 10 questions

    Aim for 7 of 10 before moving on; re-run it if the family fights back.

  6. Drill 6.3 The Distinction

    Showing precisely why an apparent precedent doesn't apply.

  2. Read Jorge Luis Borges — "The Garden of Forking Paths"

    Structure becomes the subject: the story is built like the labyrinth it describes.

  3. Quiz Narrative & Time — 10 questions

  6. Drill 8.3 The Three-Movement Structure

    Organizing any response into setup, development, and payoff.
