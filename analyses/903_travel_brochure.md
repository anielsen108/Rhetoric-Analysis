# Passage 903: The Travel Brochure

**A Regional Tourism Office** · *Discover Coastal Splendour* (a composed specimen) · 2024 | Front panel

**Occasion/Pressure:** A brochure must make one stretch of coastline sound better than the next town's identical stretch. The writer reached for every tool at once.
**Persona/Stance:** Institutional enthusiasm; the voice of no one in particular, delighted about everything.
**Thesis of Effect:** Alliteration run to mania, figures no mind can picture, and clichés laid end to end. Sound has been maximized and sense has quietly left the premises.

---

## PASSAGE

[L1] Nestled in nature's napkin of nourishing niceness, our seaside sanctuary
[L2] simply sizzles with sun-soaked, spirit-soothing splendour.
[L3] Here, memories are made, dreams come true, and every sunset paints a
[L4] picture-perfect postcard of paradise that money simply cannot buy.

---

## A) RHETORICAL TROPES USED

### **Catachresis** (the napkin)
**Lines:** L1
**Definition:** A strained or impossible figure.
**Evidence:** "nature's napkin of nourishing niceness"
**Effect:** No mind can hold this image. Is the coast folded? Is it wiping something? Good catachresis is wrong in a useful way — "the watery part of the world" — but this is only wrong. The one question it raises is *why a napkin*, and there is no answer.
**Near-miss:** Melville's catachresis defamiliarizes the sea. This one defamiliarizes napkins.

### **Hyperbole** (blanket coverage)
**Lines:** L3–4
**Definition:** Systematic overstatement.
**Evidence:** "every sunset," "paradise that money simply cannot buy"
**Effect:** *Every* sunset. The claim insures itself against no possible evening. Hyperbole persuades when it picks one thing and overstates it; spread over everything, it says nothing about anything.

---

## B) RHETORICAL SCHEMES USED

### **Alliteration** (mania)
**Lines:** L1–2
**Definition:** Repeated initial sounds in neighbouring words.
**Evidence:** "nestled...nature's napkin of nourishing niceness," "simply sizzles with sun-soaked, spirit-soothing splendour"
**Effect:** Four n-words, then six s-words, chosen for their initials rather than their meanings — "niceness" is in this sentence for no other reason. Alliteration binds two or three words; at six, the reader hears only the trick.
**Risk:** Past the third repetition, alliteration turns comic.

### **Enumeratio** (of clichés)
**Lines:** L3
**Definition:** A listed accumulation of items.
**Evidence:** "memories are made, dreams come true"
**Effect:** A list whose every item is second-hand. A list promises that each entry adds something, but clichés add nothing. Two in a row is a list of nothing.

---

## C) MODERN SYNTACTICAL APPROACHES USED

### **Intensifier Dependence**
**Lines:** L2, L4
**Definition:** Adverbs doing work the nouns and verbs have declined.
**Evidence:** "simply sizzles," "simply cannot buy"
**Effect:** "Simply," twice, simplifying nothing. The intensifier is a confession: the writer suspects the verb is weak and hopes an adverb will hold it up. It never does. Prose that trusts its verbs doesn't prop them up.

---

## 1) THE AUTOPSY

Count the decisions made by sound: napkin (for the n), niceness (for the n), sizzles (for the s), splendour (for the s). Nearly half the content words were chosen by their first letters. That is the core problem: a sentence assembled sound-first, meaning-second. The clichés then finish the job: where the alliteration was at least energetic, "memories are made" is a phrase that has never made anyone feel anything, which is why committees trust it.

## 2) THE REPAIR

Name things. *"The beach is a mile of white sand. The water is warm until October. At sunset, the whole bay turns copper."* Three facts, one image, no adverbs. Specific detail is the only thing a brochure can offer that a reader believes.
